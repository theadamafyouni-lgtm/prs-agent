## The one idea that connects all three questions

Continental ancestry *labels* are a lossy discretization of a continuous genetic space. This single fact drives all three answers: it is why PCA-based similarity works at all (Q1), why continental inference survives sparse assays while fine-scale inference does not (Q2), and why self-reported and inferred ancestry disagree most for admixed people — the same individuals a discrete classifier is least sure about (Q3). The admixed case is therefore not a separate edge case bolted onto the end; it is the shared failure mode, and it is threaded through Q1 and Q3 rather than siloed.

# Q1 — How genetic ancestry is inferred by similarity to reference populations

*Review section for the PRS-agent requirements spec. Every factual claim carries an inline resolvable citation (DOI/stable URL). Passages marked **[INFERRED]** are extrapolations beyond what the cited source states; passages marked **[Not established in retrieved literature]** flag genuine gaps.*

## Scope and framing

The interpret stage of the agent needs one thing from ancestry inference: a way to place a query genome next to an ancestry-matched slice of the population reference so that a polygenic score can be read against the right distribution. This is a *similarity-to-reference* problem, not a genealogy or identity problem. Nearly all production pipelines solve it the same way — reduce a labelled reference panel to a low-dimensional genetic space (almost always principal components), place the query genome in that space, and either read off a discrete group label with a classifier or keep continuous coordinates. This section surveys those methods, the tools that implement them, and how each is calibrated, and then treats the case the discrete-label machinery handles worst: admixed individuals.

## Similarity-to-reference methods

### Principal-component space as substrate

The reason PCA underpins almost every ancestry method is that the top principal components of genome-wide genotypes recover geographic and continental structure directly from the data. [Novembre et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature07566) showed that for European samples the first two PCs of ~500,000 SNPs reproduce a recognizable map of Europe — genes mirror geography — which is the empirical basis for treating PC coordinates as an ancestry summary. The continental reference panels the field uses were built for exactly this: the 1000 Genomes Project provides genome sequences across 26 populations grouped into five continental superpopulations (AFR, AMR, EAS, EUR, SAS) as "a global reference for human genetic variation" ([1000 Genomes Project Consortium 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature15393)), and the Human Genome Diversity Project panel adds 929 genomes from 54 geographically diverse populations ([Bergström et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay5012)). gnomAD and All of Us both build their ancestry labels on the combined HGDP + 1000 Genomes reference, a harmonized deeply-sequenced resource described by [Koenig et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.278378.123).

The core mechanical distinction is between **computing** PCs on a dataset and **projecting** a new sample onto PCs already computed on a reference. Projection is what a per-individual product does: the reference PC space is fixed, and each query genome is placed into it without recomputing the decomposition. The projection step is where most of the statistical subtlety lives (see *Calibration* below).

### Projection methods and their bias

The simplest projection multiplies the query's genotypes by the reference PC loadings. [Privé et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btaa520) state that this "simple projection" suffers a **shrinkage bias** — projected coordinates are pulled toward zero (the origin) relative to where the sample would sit had it been part of the original PCA — and that in the 1000 Genomes reference this bias "becomes large for PC5 and beyond." This matters directly for the agent: an admixed or non-European query projected with naive loadings is biased toward the centre of the reference cloud, which can make it look more "central"/European-like than it is. Privé et al. provide an unbiased-projection implementation in the R packages `bigsnpr`/`bigutilsr`.

LASER and TRACE attack the same bias differently, by re-running PCA in an augmented dataset for each sample and aligning with a Procrustes transform (the "data augmentation, decomposition and Procrustes", ADP, approach). [Wang et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.2924) introduced LASER for placing sequence reads (including very low-coverage data) into a reference PC space by simulating reference-like genotypes from the reads; [Wang et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajhg.2015.04.018) extended it (TRACE / projection Procrustes) to handle both genotyping and sequencing data via genotype imputation into the reference. The trade-off is cost: [Zhang et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btaa152) report that ADP methods such as LASER/TRACE require running PCA separately per study sample, and that for the UK Biobank (488,366 samples, 1000 Genomes reference) the projected ADP computation time was ~1,628 CPU hours.

FRAPOSA ([Zhang et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btaa152)) was built to get ADP-quality (unbiased) projections at projection-method speed. It implements four options — simple projection, ADP, a bias-adjusted projection (AP) that estimates the shrinkage analytically via random-matrix theory, and an online ADP (OADP) — and reports that AP and OADP are unbiased while being "16–16,000 times faster than ADP" (AP: 0.82 CPU h, OADP: 21 CPU h on the same UK Biobank task). The paper also states that when inferring *sub-European* ancestry the simple projection "clearly showed bias" whereas the corrected approaches did not — i.e. the finer the distinction the agent needs, the more the projection correction matters. **[INFERRED]** For a per-individual product, the ADP per-sample cost is irrelevant (there is one sample), so the practical choice is between simple projection with a known bias and a bias-corrected projection (AP/OADP or `bigsnpr`); the sources establish the bias and the corrections but do not benchmark the single-query per-individual case.

### Classification on PC coordinates

Once a query sits in reference PC space, a discrete label comes from a supervised classifier trained on the labelled reference samples. The **All of Us** pipeline is the most directly relevant instance because All of Us is the settled population reference. [All of Us Research Program Genomics Investigators 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06957-x) state that they "trained a random forest classifier on a training set of the HGDP and 1000 Genomes samples," using "the first 16 principal components (PCs) of the training sample genotypes (using the `hwe_normalized_pca` in Hail)" as the feature vector, with truth labels from sample metadata; they then "project the All of Us samples into the PCA space of the training data and apply the classifier." The ancestry categories are the gnomAD/HGDP/1000 Genomes labels: AFR, AMR (Latino/admixed American), EAS, MID (Middle Eastern), EUR (with FIN/NFE subgroups), SAS, and OTH ("not belonging to one of the other ancestries or is an admixture"). This is the canonical similarity-to-reference recipe: reference PCA → project query → classify.

gnomAD uses the same family of methods and originated the label set. [Karczewski et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2308-7) is the gnomAD flagship describing ancestry assignment by PCA plus a random-forest classifier trained on samples with known ancestry, and [Chen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06045-0) is the gnomAD v3-era (76,156 genomes) constraint/resource paper on the same pipeline lineage. **[INFERRED]** The AoU paper cites gnomAD as the source of both the label taxonomy and the training approach; that the two pipelines are methodologically the same family is stated by AoU's own citations, but the exact gnomAD probability cutoff (commonly reported as a random-forest posterior-probability threshold, e.g. assigning "Other" when no group exceeds a cutoff) is **[Not established in the passages retrieved here]** — it lives in the gnomAD methods/blog documentation, which should be cited at run time rather than asserted.

Other classifiers occupy the same slot. **peddy** ([Pedersen & Quinlan 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajhg.2017.01.017)) is designed for sequencing QC: it projects a sample onto 1000 Genomes PCs and uses a machine-learning classifier on the projected PCs to predict continental ancestry and flag pedigree/sex anomalies — relevant to the agent because it is a widely-used, lightweight ancestry+QC check on VCFs. **[Not established in retrieved sources]** The specific classifier algorithm (e.g. SVM vs k-nearest-neighbour) was not verified against the peddy paper's full text in this session and should be read from the tool documentation before being asserted. **GRAF-pop** ([Jin et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1534/g3.118.200925)) deliberately avoids PCA: it uses geometric/probabilistic distances on ancestry-informative genotypes, and its stated advantage is robustness to *non-randomly missing genotypes* — it "gives much better ancestry predictions than the PCA-based methods" (EIGENSTRAT, FastPCA, FlashPCA2) when genotypes are sparse or drawn from heterogeneous platforms, while being comparable when genotypes are dense. GRAF-pop has been part of the dbGaP QC pipeline since 2018, used specifically to reconcile study-reported vs computationally-predicted population. **[INFERRED — directly load-bearing for the assay-class constraint]** GRAF-pop's robustness-to-missingness result is the clearest published signal that the *pattern* of absent genotypes (platform-driven, non-random) changes ancestry-inference accuracy — which is exactly why the agent cannot treat "missing" identically across WGS/WES/array/gVCF; the source establishes that missingness structure degrades PCA-based inference and that a missingness-robust method exists, but does not analyze the four assay classes as such.

**SNPweights** ([Chen et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btt144)) implements the reference-panel-weights idea that a per-individual product depends on: precomputed SNP weights (loadings) from a large reference let you infer a new sample's ancestry *without* re-analyzing the reference's raw genotypes each time. Its reported accuracy is a strong calibration anchor: in 1,636 European-American GWAS samples it attained PC-prediction R² of 1.000 and 0.994 for the first two PCs using reference weights, versus 0.418/0.407 from 150 published ancestry-informative markers and 0.955/0.003 from applying PCA directly to the (small) target sample. **[INFERRED]** This quantifies why the product should project against a large fixed reference rather than run PCA on a single genome — a single query cannot support its own PCA — but the R²=1.000 figure is for a European-American cohort, not a per-individual admixed case.

### Model-based methods (for comparison)

A separate family estimates ancestry *fractions* under an explicit population-genetic model rather than by geometric similarity. **STRUCTURE** ([Pritchard et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/155.2.945)) introduced Bayesian MCMC inference of population structure and per-individual admixture proportions from multilocus genotypes. **ADMIXTURE** ([Alexander et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.094052.109)) computes the same maximum-likelihood admixture proportions far faster, and supports a *supervised* mode where reference individuals are fixed to known populations — the model-based analogue of similarity-to-reference. **fastNGSadmix** ([Jørsboe et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btx474)) estimates admixture proportions and PCA placement for a *single* NGS sample, including low-depth data, using reference panel allele frequencies — the model-based method closest to the per-individual product use case. **[INFERRED]** These return continuous admixture fractions rather than a single label, which is more faithful for admixed queries, but they are slower and require choosing K (number of ancestral components); the sources establish the methods, not a head-to-head recommendation for the agent.

## Admixture and the discordance problem

### Why discrete labels break

Admixed individuals are the case discrete assignment handles worst, and the reason is structural, not a tuning failure. An individual with, say, substantial African and European ancestry projects to an *intermediate* position in PC space — between the AFR and EUR reference clouds — where no single continental label is correct. All of Us encodes this directly: the AMR ("Latino/admixed American") and OTH categories explicitly absorb individuals who are admixtures or do not sit cleanly in one group ([All of Us 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06957-x)). The same paper reports that concordance between self-reported ethnicity and predicted genetic ancestry was **87.7%** — i.e. ~1 in 8 participants' genetic-ancestry prediction did not match their self-report. **[INFERRED]** The discordance the project is worried about (self-reported vs inferred, and inferred-label instability) shares one root cause with the admixture problem: continental *labels* are a lossy discretization of a continuous genetic space, so any individual near a boundary — admixed, recently migrated, or from an underrepresented population between reference clusters — can be labelled differently by different pipelines or disagree with self-report, without any method being "wrong." The 87.7% figure is what the source states; the causal framing is our synthesis.

Three responses to this appear in the literature, and they map cleanly onto the agent's options:

1. **Global admixture fractions** instead of one label — ADMIXTURE/STRUCTURE/fastNGSadmix (above) return per-individual proportions (e.g. 60% AFR / 40% EUR), which is faithful for admixed people but leaves open how to pick an ancestry-matched PRS reference from a mixture.
2. **Local ancestry inference (LAI)** — **RFMix** ([Maples et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajhg.2013.06.020)) assigns ancestry *along the chromosome*, segment by segment, using a discriminative (conditional random field / random-forest) model, so an admixed genome is described as a mosaic of ancestral tracts rather than a single label or a single global fraction. This is the highest-resolution answer to admixture. **[INFERRED]** LAI is the most faithful representation but the heaviest, and mapping a per-segment mosaic onto a single PRS reference distribution is not something the retrieved sources address.
3. **Continuous PC coordinates as the representation** — keep the query's (bias-corrected) PC coordinates and match to the reference in that continuous space, rather than forcing a discrete label at all. [Privé 2020 (preprint)](https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.10.06.328203) argues explicitly for ancestry *inference and grouping* from PCA that can express distance-to-reference continuously, and [Privé 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btac348) uses the UK Biobank as a global reference to measure continuous ancestry composition from summary statistics. All of Us itself keeps a continuous representation alongside the labels: it computed per-individual genetic-ancestry **fractions** on the first 16 PCs using the **Rye** program ([Conley et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkad149)), defining six reference groups from HGDP + 1000 Genomes ([All of Us 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06957-x)). **[INFERRED]** The continuous-coordinate route is the one that avoids forcing an admixed query into a wrong bin, but the retrieved sources establish the representation, not a validated procedure for reading a PRS against a continuous ancestry position.

## Reference panels and calibration

### Group definitions, PC counts, cutoffs

**How panels define groups.** The 1000 Genomes five superpopulations and HGDP's 54 populations are the atoms; gnomAD, All of Us and Koenig et al. harmonize HGDP + 1000 Genomes into a single labelled reference and train on it ([1000 Genomes 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature15393); [Bergström et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay5012); [Koenig et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.278378.123); [All of Us 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06957-x)). All of Us's six coherent groups (African, East Asian, European, Middle Eastern, Latino/admixed American, South Asian) are stated to correspond to participant self-identified race/ethnicity groups ([All of Us 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06957-x)) — a design choice, not a biological given.

**How prediction is calibrated.** The recurring recipe: (i) train the classifier / compute PC loadings on the *reference*, not the query; (ii) fix a number of PCs — All of Us uses **16** ([All of Us 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06957-x)), and Privé et al. recommend **16–18** PCs for the UK Biobank, having found that higher PCs (PC19–PC40) capture long-range LD structure rather than population structure ([Privé et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btaa520)); (iii) correct projection shrinkage (AP/OADP or `bigsnpr`) ([Zhang et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btaa152); [Privé et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btaa520)); (iv) apply a cutoff for the "Other"/uncertain bin. Point (iv) — the explicit probability/posterior cutoff and how "Other" is assigned — is **[Not established in the passages retrieved here]** for either gnomAD or All of Us; it should be read from the live gnomAD/AoU methods documentation at run time.

**Accuracy metrics each method reports** (as stated by the sources):
- SNPweights: PC-prediction R² up to 1.000 / 0.994 (PC1/PC2) in European-American samples using reference weights ([Chen et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btt144)).
- All of Us: 87.7% concordance between self-reported ethnicity and predicted genetic ancestry ([All of Us 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06957-x)).
- FRAPOSA: unbiasedness of AP/OADP projections and 16–16,000× speedup over ADP ([Zhang et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btaa152)).
- GRAF-pop: comparable to PCA methods on dense genotypes, superior on sparse/non-randomly-missing genotypes ([Jin et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1534/g3.118.200925)).
- Privé et al.: 16–18 UKB PCs capture true population structure; simple projection shrinkage grows from PC5 on the 1000 Genomes reference ([Privé et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btaa520)).

None of these is a per-individual clinical accuracy figure for the specific "one consumer genome file → ancestry-matched PRS reference" task; all are cohort- or biobank-level validations. **[Not established in retrieved literature]** — a validated per-query accuracy for consumer-grade single genomes across assay classes.

## Implications for the product

### Connection to the assay-class constraint

The methods above assume a set of reference SNPs is observed in the query. What is observed depends on the assay, and the ancestry step inherits the project's load-bearing rule that an absent position is not one thing. The clearest published hook is GRAF-pop's result that PCA-based ancestry inference degrades under *non-random* missingness and that a missingness-robust method recovers accuracy ([Jin et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1534/g3.118.200925)); LASER/TRACE's design for low-coverage sequence reads ([Wang et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.2924); [Wang et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajhg.2015.04.018)) and fastNGSadmix's for single low-depth NGS samples ([Jørsboe et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btx474)) show the field already builds assay-aware projection variants. **[INFERRED]** These establish that missingness *pattern* (not just rate) changes ancestry accuracy and that projection can be adapted to the data type; they do **not** provide a per-assay-class (WGS vs WES vs array vs gVCF) treatment of ancestry-SNP absence — that specific analysis is **[Not established in retrieved literature]** and is a genuine gap the spec should record rather than paper over.

### Why ancestry inference is in the pipeline

The reason ancestry inference is in the pipeline is that PRS models are keyed to genetic ancestry and mis-transfer across ancestries: [Martin et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-019-0379-x) show that polygenic scores derived largely from European-ancestry GWAS predict poorly in other ancestries and warn their clinical use may exacerbate disparities, and [Sirugo et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2019.02.048) document the underlying underrepresentation of non-European populations in genetic studies. Reading a score against an ancestry-matched reference distribution is the mitigation the interpret stage implements — which is why the similarity-to-reference machinery above, and its failure mode for admixed individuals, is load-bearing rather than cosmetic.

# Q2 — Does inference hold across assay classes?

## 1. Marker density and marker overlap versus inference accuracy

The central empirical finding across the ancestry-inference methods literature is that **continental-scale resolution is achievable from a small number of well-chosen markers, while fine-scale (sub-continental) resolution requires either dense genome-wide data or explicit modelling**. Two bodies of work establish this: ancestry-informative-marker (AIM) panels at the low-density end, and genome-wide / projection methods at the high-density end.

At the sparse end, dedicated AIM panels resolve continental origin with dozens to ~100 markers. Kosoy and colleagues built and validated a 128-SNP AIM set and reported that **subsets as small as 24 AIMs remain useful for ascertaining continental origin and for correcting population stratification in admixed samples** ([Kosoy 2009](https://doi.org/10.1002/humu.20822)). A 93-SNP subset of the same family distinguished continental groups across Human Genome Diversity Panel populations spanning Oceania, South Asia, East Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, the Americas and Europe, and could further separate some sub-continental groups (e.g. Ashkenazi from Northern European) ([Nassir 2009](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2156-10-39)). Forensic-genetics work converged on similarly compact panels: Kidd and colleagues report a **55-SNP panel evaluated across 73 worldwide populations** for ancestry inference, while noting that such compact panels have "limitations in their generality and efficiency" and that some panels validated on few populations do not generalise ([Kidd 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fsigen.2014.01.002)). *Source says:* a few dozen purpose-selected AIMs suffice for continental assignment. **[INFERRED]** for the product: an *arbitrary* set of a few dozen overlapping markers between a query and a reference is **not** equivalent to a purpose-selected AIM panel — the panels above were optimised for between-continent allele-frequency divergence, so their marker count is a lower bound only when the shared markers are themselves informative, not a guarantee for any sparse intersection.

That distinction is made quantitative by Chen and colleagues (SNPweights). Using genome-wide SNP weights derived from external reference panels, they attained principal-component prediction accuracy (R²) of **1.000 and 0.994 for PC1 and PC2** in 1,636 independent European-American GWAS samples, **compared with 0.418 and 0.407 using 150 published AIMs**, and showed the weighted genome-wide approach was substantially more accurate than either an AIM panel or PCA on target genotypes without a reference ([Chen 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btt144)). *Source says:* for a given task, more informative markers (genome-wide, weighted against a large reference) beat a small AIM panel by a wide margin on the top PCs. This is the core density-versus-accuracy signal for the "interpret" stage: the number of markers matters, but their information content and their calibration against the reference matter more.

The high-density methods also define how *low* coverage can go before continental resolution fails. LASER estimates ancestry by projecting a sample onto a reference PCA built from a panel and reports that it **infers worldwide continental ancestry accurately with whole-genome shotgun coverage as low as 0.001×, and fine-scale within-Europe ancestry at 0.1×** ([Wang 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.2924)). The clear asymmetry — two orders of magnitude (100×) more data needed for fine-scale than for continental — is the single most useful quantitative anchor for this track: *continental inference is data-cheap; sub-continental inference is data-expensive.*

Genotyping arrays and consumer files sit between the AIM-panel and WGS extremes. Verlouw and colleagues compared 28 genotyping arrays and found that **genome-wide coverage correlates with the number of SNVs on the array but does not correlate with imputation quality** ([Verlouw 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41431-021-00917-7)) — i.e. raw marker count is not a sufficient statistic even within the array class. **Not established in retrieved literature:** a direct, head-to-head accuracy comparison of *continental* ancestry inference on a normalised consumer-genotyping VCF (23andMe/AncestryDNA-style, ~600–700k markers) versus matched WGS from the same individuals. The retrieved sources establish that continental inference is easy at far lower information content than an array provides (Wang 2014), which makes reliable continental assignment from a consumer VCF **plausible [INFERRED]**, but the specific comparison is not published in what we retrieved.

## 2. Assay-aware and shared-marker approaches

Every projection-based ancestry method in the retrieved literature works by placing the query sample into a **reference PCA coordinate system**, and each must reconcile the fact that the query and reference rarely carry the same marker set. The methods differ in how they do this, and the differences are exactly what an assay-aware pipeline must respect.

LASER/TRACE established the projection framework. LASER 1.0 projects a sample onto a reference PCA from sequence reads ([Wang 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.2924)); LASER 2.0 / TRACE generalised it with a **projection Procrustes analysis** so that **either genotyping or sequencing data** can be placed in a shared reference space, and reported accurate fine-scale European ancestry from exome-chip genotypes or targeted sequencing with off-target coverage as low as 0.05× **when combined with genotype imputation on the reference individuals** ([Wang 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajhg.2015.04.018)). *Source says:* handling heterogeneous assays (array vs sequence, sparse vs dense) in one reference space is a solved methodological problem, and imputing the reference to a common set of sites is part of how it is solved.

FRAPOSA formalised and benchmarked the projection step itself. It implements simple projection (SP), the data-augmentation–decomposition–Procrustes (ADP) transformation used by LASER/TRACE, and two new approaches — bias-adjusted projection (AP) and online ADP (OADP). The key robustness result: **simple projection of PC loadings is biased toward the null (samples pulled toward the reference centre), and this bias becomes visible precisely when inferring sub-European (fine-scale) ancestry**, whereas AP and OADP correct it; the method was applied to 488,366 UK Biobank samples projected onto a 2,492-sample 1000 Genomes reference ([Zhang 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btaa152)). **[INFERRED]** for the product: the shrinkage-toward-null bias is worst when the query has *fewer* usable markers, because sparser data yields a less-constrained projection — so the choice of projection method (bias-corrected vs naive) matters more as the query assay gets sparser. The sources demonstrate the bias and its fine-scale sensitivity; the explicit "worse with fewer shared markers" coupling is our extrapolation.

Imputation to a common panel is the other route to a shared marker set. The Michigan Imputation Server standardised cluster-free access to large reference panels and phasing/imputation at reduced computational cost ([Das 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.3656)); the TOPMed programme provides a genotype-imputation server backed by 53,831 diverse deeply-sequenced genomes ([Taliun 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03205-y)). Imputation is not neutral, however: Sengupta and colleagues showed that in sub-Saharan African data the **best reference panel depends on the sample's own ancestry composition** — a ~20-fold smaller but ancestrally matched panel (African Genome Resource) gave *higher* concordance with WGS than a much larger panel, and concordance was strongly modulated by the extent of Khoe-San ancestry ([Sengupta 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xgen.2023.100332)). *Source says:* imputing to a "common" panel introduces an ancestry-dependent bias of its own, so imputation is not a free harmonisation step. Tooling now folds imputation and scoring together — the Imputation Server PGS extension imputes and then computes polygenic scores on the server ([Forer 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkae331)) — and the PGS Catalog Calculator (`pgsc_calc`) performs variant harmonisation as part of reproducible score calculation ([Lambert 2024](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.05.29.24307783)).

### Meaning of an absent position per assay

The retrieved sources support the individual assay facts but **do not** contain a single publication that states the four-way contrast the product relies on. We therefore mark the mapping itself as **[INFERRED]** synthesis over primary/format-level facts:

- **WGS** — a position absent from the call set is a site the assay *could* have measured; absence is closest to "reference-allele / not variant" or a coverage/QC gap, not "outside the assay's reach." (Format-level; grounded generally by whole-genome variant catalogues such as [Taliun 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03205-y).)
- **WES** — absence outside the exome capture is *structural*: the site was never targeted. gnomAD reports exome and genome call sets side by side and they differ in exactly this way — exomes carry coding-biased content ([Karczewski 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2308-7)). *Source says:* gnomAD spans both exomes and genomes; **[INFERRED]:** an ancestry-informative marker outside coding regions is systematically unmeasurable in WES, biasing any naive intersection toward coding SNPs.
- **Genotyping array / consumer VCF** — absence means "not on the chip." Array content is fixed by design and coverage tracks SNV count but not downstream imputation quality ([Verlouw 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41431-021-00917-7)); consumer files are a normalised subset of an array.
- **gVCF** — absence is *calling-dependent*: a position may fall inside a reference block (confidently non-variant) or be genuinely uncalled, and the two are distinguishable only from the block records. **Not established in retrieved literature** as a cited claim — this is a property of the gVCF/GATK joint-genotyping format, not of a paper we retrieved; the manuscript should cite the format specification directly.

**[INFERRED] consequence for safe marker intersection:** because an absent position is unmeasured (WGS), out-of-capture (WES), off-chip (array), or reference-block/uncalled (gVCF), the query↔reference intersection cannot be built by treating every absent marker identically. A shared-marker projection is only safe when absence is resolved to genotype state per assay class before intersecting — otherwise the projection conflates "reference-homozygous" with "unobserved," which shifts the sample in PC space. The projection methods above (LASER/TRACE, FRAPOSA) assume the query's observed markers are genuine calls; the sources do not test them on assay-mixed inputs where absence is ambiguous.

## 3. Degrade-versus-reliable synthesis

Synthesising the retrieved evidence, the answer is assay-dependent and resolution-dependent:

**Continental-scale inference stays reliable across assay classes and degrades gracefully with density.** The strongest single datum is that LASER recovers continental ancestry at 0.001× WGS coverage ([Wang 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.2924)) — orders of magnitude below any array or consumer file — and that a few dozen purpose-selected AIMs suffice ([Kosoy 2009](https://doi.org/10.1002/humu.20822); [Nassir 2009](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2156-10-39)). **[INFERRED]:** since arrays and consumer VCFs carry ~500k–900k markers — far more information than these lower bounds — continental assignment from these assays should be reliable *provided the shared-marker intersection with the reference is itself informative and absence is handled per assay class.*

**Fine-scale (sub-continental) inference degrades and is bias-prone on sparse inputs, and requires an assay-aware pipeline.** Wang 2014 needed 100× more coverage for within-Europe than for continental resolution; FRAPOSA shows naive projection is biased toward the null exactly at the fine-scale level ([Zhang 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btaa152)); and imputation harmonisation carries its own ancestry-dependent bias ([Sengupta 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xgen.2023.100332)). Because PRS interpretation is keyed to *continental-scale* genetic-ancestry groups (a settled project constraint), the product operates mostly in the regime where inference is robust — but any drift toward finer reference strata inherits the fragility above.

**An assay-aware pipeline is required, not optional, for two reasons the sources support.** First, the harmonisation choices (which reference, impute-or-intersect, which projection estimator) are not neutral — each carries a documented, ancestry- or density-dependent bias ([Sengupta 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xgen.2023.100332); [Zhang 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btaa152)). Second, downstream, genotype imputation alone introduces measurable variability into polygenic-score estimates relative to a WGS gold standard ([Chen 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13073-020-00801-x)), and PGS accuracy itself varies continuously along the genetic-ancestry continuum at the individual level ([Ding 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06079-4)) — so an ancestry-placement error propagates into the risk estimate rather than staying contained in the "interpret" stage.

### Gaps flagged (not established in retrieved literature)

1. **Head-to-head continental-ancestry-inference accuracy on a normalised consumer VCF (23andMe/AncestryDNA txt) vs matched WGS from the same individuals.** Retrieved sources make it plausible (Wang 2014) but do not publish the comparison.
2. **A single citable source for the four-way "absent-position" semantics** (WGS-unmeasured / WES-out-of-capture / array-off-chip / gVCF-reference-block). This is synthesis over format-level facts; the gVCF component in particular has no paper in our retrieval and should cite the format specification.
3. **Minimum informative-marker count for four/five-way continental resolution specifically on a consumer VCF intersected against the All of Us reference.** AIM-panel lower bounds exist for their own reference sets ([Kosoy 2009](https://doi.org/10.1002/humu.20822); [Kidd 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fsigen.2014.01.002)) but not for an arbitrary consumer↔All-of-Us intersection.
4. **Robustness of LASER/TRACE/FRAPOSA projection when query absence is assay-ambiguous** (mixed reference-homozygous vs unobserved). The methods were validated on genuine calls, not on assay-mixed absence.

# Q3 — Reconciling inferred with self-reported ancestry

**Scope.** This section covers (1) primary evidence on how well self-reported race/ethnicity agrees with genetically inferred ancestry, (2) how the literature and tools define a *meaningful* disagreement, and whether the two are combined for downstream PRS ancestry-matching, and (3) how existing tools present inferred ancestry and any conflict to non-experts. Throughout, claims a source explicitly makes are stated plainly with an inline DOI; anything we extrapolate for the product is marked **[INFERRED]**. Where retrieved primary literature does not answer a question, it is marked **not established in retrieved literature**.

The organizing thread: **self-report and genetically inferred ancestry disagree most for admixed people**, and that is not a coincidence: it is the same phenomenon as continental labels losing meaning at the individual level. The two are not measurements of one quantity. Self-reported race/ethnicity is a social and cultural construct; genetically inferred ancestry describes similarity to reference genomes. A product that treats a mismatch between them as an "error" in one or the other has mis-modeled the problem.

## 1. Concordance evidence

At the level of continental self-identification in well-designed US cohorts, agreement is very high. In the Family Blood Pressure Program sample, genetic cluster analysis of 326 microsatellite markers produced four clusters showing near-perfect correspondence with self-identified white, African American, East Asian, and Hispanic groups; of 3,636 subjects, only 5 (0.14%) clustered differently from their self-identified race/ethnicity ([Tang et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1086/427888)). This is the landmark "high concordance" result, and it is genuinely high. Note the design: four maximally separated continental groups, a population-based recruitment, and self-identification into pre-set categories. Tang et al. also found that ancient geographic ancestry, highly correlated with self-identified race/ethnicity, rather than current residence, is the major determinant of genetic structure in the US population ([Tang et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1086/427888)).

The GERA/Kaiser Permanente cohort (103,006 participants) reproduces high aggregate concordance while exposing where it frays. Among genetically identified parent–child pairs, 93% were concordant for self-reported race/ethnicity, and among full-sib pairs 96%; the authors attribute the lower parent–child rate largely to intermarriage and note a trend toward increasing exogamy over time ([Banda et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1534/genetics.115.178616)). Crucially, the same study quantifies the admixed margin directly: roughly 17% of subjects had genetic ancestry from more than one continent and ~12% were genetically admixed (considering only nonadjacent geographical origins); self-reported African Americans and Latinos showed extensive European and African ancestry, with Native American ancestry for the latter, and 6.2% of the cohort endorsed two or more race/ethnicity categories ([Banda et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1534/genetics.115.178616)). The concordance number and the admixture number are the same finding read two ways: agreement is high precisely where groups are endogamous and separated, and it becomes ill-defined, not merely lower, where individuals carry multi-continental ancestry.

That breakdown is sharpest in admixed Hispanic/Latino and African American populations, which is exactly where PRS portability is worst. Among Mexican and Puerto Rican asthma case-control subjects, ancestry-informative markers revealed population stratification substantial enough to confound association studies, requiring explicit correction; a single self-reported "Latino" label conceals a continuum of European, African, and Native American ancestry proportions ([Choudhry et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00439-005-0071-3)). The clinical-records analogue: in the Vanderbilt BioVU biorepository, the study set out to test whether administratively assigned (observer-reported) race/ethnicity from electronic medical records can serve as a proxy for genetic ancestry, using ancestry-informative SNPs, establishing that administrative race labels cannot be assumed to track genetic ancestry and must be checked ([Dumitrescu et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1097/GIM.0b013e3181efe2df)). **[INFERRED]** For our product this matters because a genome file may arrive with a self-reported label of unknown provenance (self-entered vs clinic-assigned); the literature does not license treating either as ground truth for ancestry.

The conceptual point is made explicitly in review. Mersha & Abebe distinguish the terms: race refers to physical appearance, ethnicity to shared cultural heritage and language, and both are separate axes from genetic ancestry; the review examines the limitations of self-reported race/ethnicity as a stand-in for ancestry in genomic research ([Mersha & Abebe 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40246-014-0023-x)). The 2023 US National Academies consensus report formalizes the field's move away from race as a genetic variable, providing a framework for using population descriptors in genetics and genomics research ([NASEM 2023](https://doi.org/10.17226/26902)); genetic-counseling-research audits echo how inconsistently race, ethnicity, and ancestry are collected and reported in practice ([Arpone et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/jgc4.1884)), and commentators argue that multiracial individuals, the group where discordance concentrates, must be explicitly included rather than dropped from race/ethnicity/ancestry frameworks ([Martschenko et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-023-01394-y)).

**Bottom line for section 1.** Concordance is high at continental scale in endogamous, well-separated groups (approx. 99.9% in Tang; 93-96% in GERA family pairs) and degrades in a *structured*, predictable way in admixed populations, not as noise but because the self-reported label and the genetic-ancestry axis stop corresponding when ancestry is mixed.

## 2. Thresholds and weighting

**How tools define a genetic-ancestry group and a "confident" call.** The dominant operational pattern is: project a target genome into a principal-component (PC) space defined by a labeled reference panel, then classify. All of Us trained a random-forest classifier on a 16-dimensional PCA space of a diverse reference (3,202 samples, ~151,159 autosomal SNPs from HGDP + 1000 Genomes), projected participant WGS into that space, and generated categorical ancestry predictions plus continuous ancestry fractions; its category set is inherited from gnomAD: AFR, AMR, EAS, MID, EUR (FIN + NFE), SAS, and **Other (OTH)**, the last defined as "not belonging to one of the other ancestries or is an admixture" ([All of Us 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06957-x)). The handling of admixture is explicit and load-bearing: the classifier is *not* trained on samples labeled Other; instead the label probabilities ("confidence") of the classifier on the defined ancestries are used to decide when a sample falls into Other ([All of Us 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06957-x)). This is the mechanism by which admixed individuals are handled. They are the ones a discrete classifier is least confident about, and they are routed to a catch-all rather than forced into a continental box.

The same PC-projection-plus-classifier design underlies the gnomAD-style resource: a random-forest classifier is trained on HGDP + 1000 Genomes region labels and applied to projected target data to assign genetic-ancestry groups ([Koenig et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.278378.123)). **Not established in retrieved literature:** the *specific* random-forest posterior-probability cutoff (commonly cited as ~0.75 / 0.9, above which a sample is assigned to a group and below which it is left unassigned/Other) is documented in gnomAD's online production documentation, but I could not extract that numeric threshold from the primary papers retrieved here. It should be cited to gnomAD's method documentation at run time, not asserted from these sources. **[INFERRED]** The general design pattern is clear from the retrieved papers; the exact number is not.

**The trend the PRS field is actually taking: away from discrete labels toward continuous ancestry / PC-distance.** This is the most important finding for the interpret stage. Ding et al. state it directly: homogeneous populations are an idealized concept that only roughly approximates human data, which exist along a **genetic ancestry continuum** without clearly defined clusters; grouping individuals into discrete genetically-inferred-ancestry clusters *obscures* the impact of individual variation on PGS accuracy ([Ding et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06079-4)). They operationalize a continuous **genetic distance (GD)**, the Euclidean distance between a target individual and the centre of the training data in PC space, and show PGS accuracy decreases continuously along it: on average across 84 traits, accuracy drops ~14% between the furthest and closest deciles even *within* the European group; and, decisively for admixed users, GD can evaluate PGS performance for the ~6% of individuals who cannot be cleanly clustered by current inference methods ([Ding et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06079-4)). Privé et al. independently show PGS portability decays as a systematic function of genetic distance between training and test data, dropping off even within European ancestries and reducing globally in proportion to genetic distance ([Privé et al. 2022, AJHG](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajhg.2021.11.008)); the companion method defines 21 worldwide ancestry groups from the UK Biobank and measures ancestry proportions by PC projection against reference allele frequencies (implemented as `snp_ancestry_summary` in the R package `bigsnpr`) ([Privé 2022, Bioinformatics](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btac348)). The Eurocentric-bias driver is the well-established backdrop: current PRS are several times more accurate in European-ancestry individuals, an inescapable consequence of GWAS sampling bias ([Martin et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-019-0379-x)), and reviews now lay out principles for transferring PRS across global populations rather than assuming a single reference ([Kachuri et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-023-00637-2)).

**Is self-report weighted or combined with genetic ancestry for PRS matching?** The retrieved evidence says the ancestry-matching quantity for PRS is *genetic*, not self-reported, and increasingly continuous rather than categorical. All of Us uses self-reported ethnicity only as an external check on the genetic prediction (see section 3), not as the matching variable ([All of Us 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06957-x)); Ding and Privé match on PC-space distance ([Ding et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06079-4); [Privé et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajhg.2021.11.008)). In clinical implementation, eMERGE adjusts returned risk estimates with a PCA-based calibration that models both the mean and variance of the score as a function of ancestry, fit (with permission) to All of Us genotyping data to allow *continuous* return of results; the calibration is a smooth function of genetic ancestry rather than a lookup by self-reported group ([Lennon et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-02796-z)). **Not established in retrieved literature:** a documented scheme that *numerically weights* self-report against inferred ancestry (e.g., a formula that blends the two) for PRS matching: I found none; the pattern is that genetic ancestry is used for the computation and self-report is used for communication/checking, not that the two are algebraically combined. **[INFERRED]** For our product, the defensible reading is: match on genetic ancestry (ideally continuous PC-distance to the All of Us reference), and use self-report to *communicate* and to flag when the two diverge, not as an input to the score.

## 3. Lay-user presentation

**What is documented.** All of Us reports a concrete, quotable concordance figure framed as a validation of its predictions against participant self-report: the concordance between self-reported ethnicity and the ancestry predictions was 87.7% ([All of Us 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06957-x)). The lower-than-Tang figure is expected: it is measured across a diverse national cohort including many admixed participants, not four separated continental groups. All of Us also states its intent to return individual DNA results, including genetic ancestry, to participants who want them, according to clinical standards ([All of Us 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06957-x)). **Not established in retrieved literature:** the *format* in which All of Us presents genetic ancestry to a lay participant, and specifically how it presents or reconciles a case where inferred ancestry conflicts with what the participant reported: the primary paper describes the computation and the aggregate concordance, not the participant-facing report design.

**Clinical PRS reporting is where conflict-handling is most explicitly discussed.** The eMERGE network treats "how people are grouped" as a first-class communication problem, not just a statistical one: it documents the decisions and trade-offs a large multi-site study faced in returning PRS to diverse participants, precisely because differential performance by group must be conveyed honestly at return of results ([Lewis et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajhg.2024.04.005)). eMERGE notes that methods for defining population groups across the source studies ranged from self-report to extraction from health-system data to genetic-ancestry analysis, heterogeneity that itself must be managed when reporting ([Lennon et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-02796-z)). This is documented *practice-level* guidance on communicating group-dependent uncertainty to lay recipients; it is the closest retrieved analogue to our report stage.

**DTC ancestry products.** The methodological basis of 23andMe's Ancestry Composition is documented primarily in preprints. Ancestry Composition is a three-stage local-ancestry pipeline: a support-vector-machine classifier assigns tentative ancestry labels to short phased genomic regions, an autoregressive pair-HMM reconciles them into local ancestry estimates with confidence scores, and confidence estimates are recalibrated by isotonic regression against a reference panel of ~10,000 homogeneous-ancestry individuals across 25+ populations ([Durand et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1101/010512)), with a later scalable local-ancestry-inference pipeline using tens of thousands of reference haplotypes ([Durand et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.01.19.427308)). The load-bearing design feature for lay presentation is the *calibrated confidence score*: the pipeline is built to express uncertainty per segment. **Not established in retrieved literature:** the consumer-facing behavior of 23andMe's product: the conservative-to-speculative confidence slider, how continental vs sub-continental estimates are shown, and how the product frames a result that surprises a user relative to family lore. This is described in 23andMe's on-site methodology whitepaper, which I did not retrieve as a citable primary document here. AncestryDNA's consumer methodology is likewise not in the retrieved set. These should be cited to the companies' published white papers at spec time, not asserted from the preprints, which cover the algorithm and not the UI. **[INFERRED]** DTC products do surface calibrated per-segment confidence to users, but the retrieved primary sources establish the algorithm's confidence machinery, not the interface's conflict-with-self-report messaging.

**Handling admixed users specifically.** The methods literature is beginning to build PRS machinery for admixed individuals rather than excluding them: DiscoDivas interpolates PRS across the genetic-ancestry continuum specifically to serve admixed populations ([Ruan et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.11.09.24316996)), consistent with the continuum framing of Ding and Privé. **[INFERRED]** For the report stage this suggests the honest lay message for an admixed user is not "you are group X" but "your genome sits between reference groups, and the score's reliability reflects that distance," a framing the continuous-ancestry papers support statistically but which no retrieved source has validated as lay-comprehensible copy.

## Gaps (not established in retrieved literature)

1. **Exact classifier probability cutoffs.** The specific gnomAD random-forest posterior-probability threshold (~0.75/0.9) for assigning vs withholding a genetic-ancestry group label is production-documentation, not extractable from the retrieved papers. Cite gnomAD method docs at run time.
2. **A numeric self-report vs genetic-ancestry weighting scheme for PRS matching.** No retrieved source algebraically combines the two; the documented practice is genetic ancestry for computation, self-report for communication/checking.
3. **Participant-facing presentation format for genetic ancestry in All of Us**, and its explicit conflict-with-self-report messaging. The primary paper documents the computation and 87.7% aggregate concordance, not the return-of-results UI.
4. **DTC consumer-UI conflict handling** (23andMe confidence slider, AncestryDNA framing, surprising-result copy). Documented in company white papers not retrieved here; the retrieved preprints cover the algorithms only.
5. **Validated lay-comprehensible copy for communicating continuous ancestry / PC-distance uncertainty** to a genetics-naive admixed user. The statistical case for continuous framing is strong; its comprehensibility to lay readers is untested in the retrieved literature.
